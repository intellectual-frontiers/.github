# Writing Guide

> **Status: canonical.** Authored directly in this repository — this is the
> first document in `context/` or `spec-kit/` that did not start as a copy
> of something already published on `intellectualfrontiers.com`. It governs
> how anyone, human or AI, writes for Shahid Shah personally or for
> Intellectual Frontiers.

Write in Shahid Shah's voice. The writing should sound like an experienced
founder, operator, technologist, and investor speaking directly to a smart
reader. Everything below describes that voice. The only thing that changes
between a personal piece and a corporate one is the point of view.

## Whose voice, which point of view

**Personal voice — first person.** Use this for anything carrying Shahid's
own byline: essays, book chapters, the doctrine, talks, social posts,
personal correspondence made public. Use "I" for beliefs, experience,
judgment, and decisions. Use "we" when the work requires employees,
partners, advisers, investors, founders, or other people to execute it.

**Corporate voice — third person active.** Use this for anything published
as Intellectual Frontiers or one of its units without an individual byline:
unit pages, product copy, press releases, formal notices. Name the actual
entity and keep it the active subject of the sentence: "Intellectual
Frontiers stops when the evidence says stop." "IF IP checks freedom to
operate before it licenses a patent." Never slide into passive voice to
dodge picking a subject — passive voice is not a neutral substitute for a
byline, it's a way of hiding who did what.

| Personal | Corporate |
| --- | --- |
| I believe... I decided... | Shahid Shah believes... Intellectual Frontiers decided... |
| We built it. We tested it. | Intellectual Frontiers built it. IF Studios tested it. |
| My judgment. My experience. | Shahid Shah's judgment. The firm's operating record. |

Every rule below applies to both registers. Only the pronoun and the named
actor change. Where a rule below is written in "I" for readability, read it
as "the firm" or the relevant unit when you're writing in corporate voice.

## Start with the point

Do not warm up, introduce the topic, describe what the piece will cover, or
explain why the reader should care before saying what matters. Do not
summarize the argument before making it. Do not repeat the conclusion after
making it.

## Person and pronoun

Use active voice in whichever register applies (see above). In personal
voice, use "I" for beliefs, experience, judgment, and decisions, and "we"
when the work requires other people to execute it. In corporate voice, name
the entity and keep it the active subject throughout.

## Plain, specific language

Write in plain spoken, specific language. Use active verbs, concrete
examples, named situations, evidence, and consequences. Explain difficult
ideas in ordinary words without making them simplistic. Prefer a clear
assertion followed by evidence, an example, or a practical implication.

AI is encouraged to use colloquial language freely — the kinds of words and
phrasing a smart, direct person would use in conversation. Contractions,
informal phrases, and everyday expressions are welcome when they make ideas
clearer and the writing more honest. This is not a constraint; it is
permission.

## Don't hedge

Do not hedge unless uncertainty is real and important. Avoid phrases such
as:

- "It is important to note"
- "It is worth mentioning"
- "One could argue"
- "It may be helpful to consider"
- "In many ways"
- "To some extent"
- "Generally speaking"
- "There are several factors"
- "The answer is nuanced"
- "Only time will tell"

If something is uncertain, say exactly what is unknown, why it is unknown,
and what evidence would resolve it.

## No throat-clearing

Do not use AI throat-clearing or stock introductions such as:

- "In today's rapidly evolving landscape"
- "As we navigate an increasingly complex world"
- "At its core"
- "The purpose of this document is"
- "This section explores"
- "Let us begin by"
- "Before diving in"
- "In conclusion"
- "Ultimately"
- "The key takeaway is"

Do not announce content and then provide it. A heading should make the
point, not introduce a subject. Write "Native Alpha governs our decisions,"
not "Understanding the importance of Native Alpha." Write "We stop when the
evidence says stop," not "Reasons we may consider stopping."

## No performance

Do not write witty, literary, lyrical, ornate, or self-consciously polished
prose. Clever language makes practical ideas harder to follow. Do not use
metaphors, wordplay, dramatic imagery, or unusual phrasing merely to make
the writing memorable.

Humor is allowed when it makes a practical point easier to understand. Keep
it brief, natural, and attached to the argument. The reader should remember
the point, not admire the joke. Never let humor weaken a serious claim,
obscure uncertainty, or make another person the target.

## Be contrarian on purpose, not for attention

Be contrarian, but never contrarian for attention. Question accepted
wisdom, common practices, fashionable language, and inherited assumptions.
Look for what experienced people have stopped noticing. State the unobvious
fact, hidden constraint, neglected advantage, or uncomfortable implication.

A contrarian claim must lead somewhere practical. Explain what the
conventional view gets wrong, what the evidence suggests instead, and what
someone should do differently. If the opposing view is correct, say so. Do
not manufacture disagreement when ordinary advice is already right.

Prefer useful conclusions over balanced but empty summaries. Name the
decision, experiment, smallest useful test, or next action. If a bold claim
cannot change what someone builds, buys, funds, publishes, measures, or
stops doing, it probably does not belong.

## Banned vocabulary

Avoid consultant, marketing, academic, and AI language. Do not use words
such as "delve," "leverage," "unlock," "transformative," "multifaceted,"
"holistic," "journey," "paradigm," "synergy," "stakeholder alignment,"
"value proposition," or "strategic imperative." Use "ecosystem" only when
naming a real network of connected parties that cannot be described more
precisely.

Also avoid the tell-tale words of machine-drafted prose. Never use:
"load-bearing," "materially," "hygiene" as a metaphor ("context hygiene"),
"objective function," "enormously valuable," "genuinely useful to know,"
"reasonably happy," "game-changer," "it's worth noting," or "landscape"
used loosely ("the AI landscape"). These words mark writing as AI-generated
on sight. Use the plain word: easier, worth a lot, real, useful.

Do not rely on slogans, sweeping claims, inflated adjectives, clever
phrasing, forced humor, em dashes, or repetitive three-part lists. Avoid
the formula "not only X, but also Y" and the overused construction "It is
not X. It is Y." That seesaw is allowed at most once per piece, only where
the contrast really earns it; everywhere else, write an ordinary sentence
or join the two halves with a semicolon. Use contrast only when it adds
real meaning.

Never announce that a point is coming. Do not write "Here's the part that
matters," "Here is the part that stings," "This is the part that surprises
people," "Here is the whole rule," or any sentence whose only job is to set
up the next paragraph. Lead with the actual sentence instead.

Do not end paragraphs with a cutesy wrap-up aside or a wink: "deserves the
same courtesy," "wearing a different hat," "without ceremony." End on the
plain point.

## Rhythm

Keep sentences reasonably short, but do not make the prose choppy. Vary
sentence length naturally. Break up tidy three-part parallel constructions
that exist for rhythm rather than meaning. Fragments are fine when they
sound like speech. Use contractions when they sound normal. Use headings,
bullets, tables, and callouts only when they make the material easier to
understand.

Do not make the voice sound more certain than the facts allow, and do not
make it sound timid when the evidence is clear. In personal voice, that
means: I am comfortable saying what I believe, what I know, what I do not
know, what I think should happen, and what evidence could change my mind.
In corporate voice, the same rule holds with the entity as the subject: the
firm says what it knows, what it doesn't, and what would change its mind.

Every paragraph should do at least one useful job: make a claim, provide
evidence, give an example, identify an unobvious implication, explain a
consequence, state a decision, or tell the reader what to do. Delete
anything that merely sounds polished.

## Folksy, not simplistic

Lean into sounding folksy when it makes an idea easier to understand. Use
familiar words, natural contractions, practical comparisons, and everyday
expressions. Prefer the word a person would say out loud over the word
they'd write in a report: "use" not "utilize," "show" not "demonstrate,"
"enough" not "sufficient." A reader without a technical degree should feel
at home; nobody should have to reach for a dictionary. Write the way an
experienced operator might explain something across a table to another
capable person. Avoid overpoliteness or artificial formality. Sound like
someone who knows what they're talking about and is willing to say it
straight.

Folksy does not mean simplistic, cute, careless, or patronizing. Do not
speak down to the reader, overexplain basic ideas, or pretend complex
questions are easy. Assume the audience is intelligent but busy. Make the
language simpler without making the thinking shallower.

Use an informal phrase or a little humor when it removes confusion, exposes
an absurdity, or makes an important point stick. Do not use it to show
personality for its own sake. The goal is clarity, not charm. The reader
should feel respected, understand the point on the first pass, and know
what to do with it.

## Procedures, runbooks, and technical documentation: switch to ASD-STE100

When writing procedures, runbooks, how-tos, or technical documentation —
and only then — follow ASD-STE100 (Simplified Technical English), the
controlled language from asd-ste100.org (Issue 9, January 2025). Essays,
briefs, and opinion pieces stay in the normal voice above. In procedures
mode:

- One instruction per sentence. Use numbered steps for anything that
  happens in sequence.
- Keep instructions to 20 words or fewer and descriptive sentences to 25 or
  fewer.
- Active voice, present tense. Write "Remove the cover," not "The cover
  should be removed."
- One word means one thing. Pick one word for each action ("remove," never
  drifting between "remove" and "take off") and stick with it for the whole
  document.
- Use a word only in its approved sense. If the word is a verb, do not turn
  it into a noun for variety.
- Define technical nouns once and reuse them exactly. No clever variations,
  no nicknames.
- Put warnings and cautions before the step they apply to, never after.
- No implied steps, no "as needed," no guessing about who does what. Every
  step names its actor.

The full STE dictionary and rule set live in the specification; this guide
borrows its writing rules. When the reader must follow steps exactly —
safety, compliance, maintenance — completeness and exactness beat charm.

## Audit pass, every time

After completing the writing, run an audit pass before returning it. Read
the full draft and remove AI smells and AI ticks. Check for:

- Any word or phrase listed above as banned — "load-bearing," "leverage,"
  "delve," "unlock," "transformative," "landscape" used loosely, "it's
  worth noting," "materially," "game-changer," and the rest.
- Announcement sentences that set up the next paragraph instead of making
  the point: "Here's the part that…," "This section explores," "Let us
  begin by."
- The "not only X, but also Y" formula and the seesaw "It is not X. It is
  Y." used more than once.
- Cutesy wrap-up asides, literary flourishes, and metaphors added for
  style.
- Generic hedging, throat-clearing openings, and "in conclusion" closings.
- Repetitive three-part lists and tidy parallel constructions put in for
  rhythm rather than meaning.
- The wrong point of view for the piece — first person sneaking into
  corporate copy, or corporate distance sneaking into something carrying
  Shahid's own byline.

Rewrite the offending sentence in plain, direct language. If the sentence
does no useful work, delete it. Do not replace one AI-smell word with
another near-synonym; replace it with the plain word a person would say out
loud. Re-read the result once more to confirm nothing crept back in.
