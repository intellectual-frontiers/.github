**Job:** Put money behind evidence with discipline.

**Color:** Frontier Blue `#214EA2`.

**The question Capital asks:** What advantage is strong enough that scarce
capital should be placed behind it?

## Where Capital's judgment comes from

Capital does not present itself as a veteran institutional fund manager —
it is a formal investment platform still new to the firm. What is not new is
a career spent allocating scarce resources: writing angel checks, serving as
an LP in Quiet Access I, supporting client M&A, governing companies from
boards, making product and payroll decisions, and working through
acquisitions, mergers, stalled companies, weak distribution, and capital
constraints.

Private-company investments span more than a decade, some successful and
some not. Both outcomes taught how good products lose to weak
distribution, governance, reserves, timing, or an incomplete thesis.

The operator's side matters too: starting with incomplete products,
recruiting teams, facing customers, building regulated systems, working
through strategic outcomes, and serving on boards. Capital decisions land
differently once you have lived with them.

Capital uses first-principles underwriting to make demand, distribution,
governance, reserves, and return assumptions explicit, and Native Alpha to
decide whether a company has an unusual right to win. Capital does not fund
the easiest visible activity. It funds the smallest hard proof that can
change an investment decision. Neither method excuses weak evidence.

**Why it's worth the risk:** an investor would not be backing a first-time
company builder trying to become an investor. The investor would be backing a
long-time founder, operator, board member, adviser, and angel who is
formalizing practical judgment into a disciplined investment system.

## What's owned vs. what's independently checked

| Owned by Capital | Handled independently |
| --- | --- |
| Mandate, underwriting judgment, final allocation, reserves, governance, conflicts, and LP relationships | Fund administration, legal, tax, audit, valuation, compliance, and independent review |
| Naming assumptions and recording dissent | Specialist diligence and licensed opinions |
| Applying the same written standard to internal and external opportunities | Controls that make related-party and cross-vehicle decisions reviewable |

## The rule

A Studio, patent, publication, relationship, or prototype does not obligate
Capital to invest. A company does not deserve capital just because
Intellectual Frontiers created it, either — self-origination earns no
pass on the underwriting standard.

**Evidence Capital produces:** underwriting, economics, committed capital,
governance, and learning.

**Boundary:** Studio activity does not justify investment, and investment
does not prove product-market fit — see the constitution's unit-boundary
rule.

## Capital as a search function

Capital is not simply downstream of Studios, waiting for a venture to
fund. It's also a search function in its own right: form a thesis, then
look for founders and companies — sourced through IF Network or the
broader market — who are already pursuing it. If their thesis is strong,
their Native Alpha is credible, and their evidence is better than ours,
there's no need to build a competing company. Backing them is a better
use of Capital than building just because we can.

That reframes what "evidence" means for Capital before it ever writes a
check: talking to a founder is evidence. Comparing their assumptions to
ours is evidence. Seeing how much traction they've already earned is
evidence. If someone else has better customer access, better
distribution, better talent, or has simply been thinking about the
problem longer, backing them is a feature of good judgment, not a failure
of originality. Studios becomes especially important only once this
search comes up empty and the evidence still holds — see the
constitution's search-before-build principle.

## Capital sources

Capital runs on two sources: the firm's own balance sheet, and LPs and
investors brought in through IF Network. Sourcing capital relationships is
part of what Network does — it isn't limited to sourcing operators and
founders.

## The funds

Capital runs two named vehicles focused on care delivery, at different
stages and with different jobs.

**Care Delivery Fund I** holds a portfolio of ventures and shared services
built to stand alone — each can spin out independently if outside demand
is strong, or stay integrated if it isn't. Fund I's job is deliberately
narrow: prove that these reimagined pieces work together. Carrying a
company further than that is Fund II's job, not Fund I's.

**Care Delivery Fund II** is in formation. Its thesis is to back
clinician-led care-delivery companies that own their reimagined care
model, not vendors selling a tool into someone else's model. It runs on
three modes rather than one: **Invest** in a clinician founder already in
motion, **take selective control** to re-platform a cash-flowing
operator, or **build** from scratch — design, staff, and capitalize a
company from nothing. Its underwriting runs on a purpose-built engine —
cohort, geography, CMS signal, reimbursement path, revenue at stake —
distinct from generic first-principles underwriting, because care-delivery
economics run on public claims and reimbursement data in a way most
opportunities don't.

**What every company in the fund has to be true of:** it contributes to a
better end-to-end patient and caregiver experience. That's a qualifying
condition, not a nice-to-have.

**The build gate:** before Fund II incorporates a company under its
"build" mode, the operating model, the target buyer, the contract
chassis, and the unit economics have to be settled. If they cannot be
settled, the build does not start. Once it does, there's a clock: design
partners inside 60 days, a first clinical workflow live by 120, first
revenue by 180.

**What Fund II is not:** a traditional venture capital fund, a private
equity fund, an accelerator, an incubator, a consulting firm, or a
short-term financial buyer. No company in it is forced into a common
holding period, financing structure, or exit schedule.

**Four ownership modes, chosen on the merits, not a schedule:** create,
acquire and grow, own for the long term, or sell/spin out — decided on
sustainable value and impact rather than a fixed exit timeline.
