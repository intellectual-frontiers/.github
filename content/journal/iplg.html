Intellectual Product-Led Growth

By Shahid Nehal Shah

Product-Led Growth works when someone experiences real value from a product before, or instead of, being sold on it. The idea is well understood for software. It is not yet well understood for organizations whose primary assets are ideas: research, frameworks, methods, intellectual property, expertise, and decision tools rather than an app. Intellectual Product-Led Growth, IPLG, is my name for the same discipline applied to that kind of firm — a research shop, an advisory practice, a publisher, a venture studio, an IP practice, a think tank.

The product isn't always software

For a knowledge-heavy organization, the product people actually touch may be a framework, a research method, an assessment, a decision tree, a scorecard, a prompt, a template, a worksheet, a field guide, a benchmark, a diagnostic, a model, an audit, a checklist, a structured experiment, or simply a disciplined way of framing a problem. IPLG means turning assets like these into things a visitor can actually use, not just read a description of.

The operating sequence is: read, try, apply, produce an artifact, contribute evidence, reveal intent, work together. A visitor should be able to get real value before being asked for a meeting, a sales call, or even an email address.

Executable content, and why a page view is weak evidence

The mechanism IPLG runs on is what I call executable content: a piece of the site that doesn't just explain a method but lets a visitor run it, on their own case, and come away with something they made. A page view tells you almost nothing. A copied prompt, a completed assessment, a downloaded tool, a submitted workflow, a challenged thesis, a requested analysis, a licensing inquiry, a design-partner offer, or a paid experiment tells you a great deal, because each one cost the visitor something — attention, time, a real case, a piece of their own evidence.

That is the same evidentiary standard the rest of the firm runs on: a claimed advantage isn't real until it has changed an actual decision, and evidence has to cost the party offering it something real. Executable content is simply that standard applied to a website instead of to a funding decision or a hiring call.

Promotion without becoming marketing

IPLG is as much Press's discipline as it is Research & IP's or Studios'. Every intellectual product still has to be found, understood, and chosen over the alternative — it still has to be promoted, sold, and diffused. The question IPLG answers for Press specifically is how to do that without narrative covering for weak evidence, the same failure Press's own claims standard already guards against.

The answer is that the executable artifact does the selling. A worksheet a reader actually completes, a prompt they actually copy into their own tools, a scorecard they actually run against a real case, makes its own argument more convincingly than a page praising the method ever could. Diffusion happens because the thing was useful once, not because the copy was persuasive. A claim still has to be labeled as an observable fact, an opinion, a framework, or an illustration, same as anything else Press publishes — IPLG doesn't exempt a promoted product from that standard, it gives Press a better way to meet it.

This is also why a Fieldbook ships with a companion skill or MCP tool rather than only prose: the book's own promotion is the reader's own use of it. The same move that makes a research pillar's output usable makes it sellable, for the same reason — both come from treating a page view as weak evidence and a completed action as strong evidence.

Implementing it, not just believing it

IPLG is not a slide we keep in a drawer. It is already the shape of how Research & IP turns a finding into a worksheet a reader can run, how Studios turns a venture thesis into an experiment brief, and how this Journal itself is built: an article that only explains Intellectual Product-Led Growth, without anything to run, would be exactly the failure it describes. The discipline has to show up in the product, not only in the argument for it.

That means the honest test of this piece isn't whether the argument is persuasive. It's whether the pages built on it actually let a visitor complete one useful action before any commercial ask — and whether, a year from now, the record shows which of those actions people actually took.
